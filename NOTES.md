# Research notes

Observations gathered while building this, kept because they are the sort of
thing that is expensive to rediscover. Nothing here is a commitment.

## What twelve production dictionaries do

Profiled from the LibreOffice collection, plus the `en_US` that ships on most
Linux desktops. Structure is directly comparable across them because every one
answers the same questions in the same syntax.

| | entries | rule groups | rules | flags | aff | dic |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Turkish | 75,909 | 59,266 | 59,266 | num | 2.2M | 35M |
| Hungarian | 97,581 | 131 | 24,542 | char | 2.5M | 1.7M |
| Kazakh (this) | 85,794 | 330 | 19,590 | num | 0.4M | 1.6M |
| Icelandic | 193,170 | 333 | 12,949 | num | 0.3M | 2.4M |
| Polish | 348,901 | 52 | 7,410 | char | 0.3M | 5.2M |
| Czech | 261,167 | 28 | 2,713 | char | 0.1M | 3.5M |
| Russian | 146,269 | 25 | 1,581 | char | 0.07M | 3.3M |
| German | 258,218 | 32 | 473 | char | 0.02M | 4.2M |
| Greek | 828,806 | 40 | 149 | char | 0.02M | 19M |
| English | 47,553 | — | 50 | char | 0.003M | 0.5M |

Two strategies, cleanly separated. **Enumerate**: Greek lists 828,806 forms and
uses 149 rules, German 258,218 and 473. **Generate**: Hungarian, this, Turkish.
Languages with rich enough morphology do not get to choose.

### Turkish went the opposite way

The only other Turkic Hunspell dictionary, and worth studying precisely because
it rejects the design used here. Its 59,266 rule groups contain exactly one
rule each — every group is a complete pre-composed suffix chain — it uses no
continuation flags at all, and its entries carry a mean of 81 flags each, up to
3,926. The `.dic` is 35 MB.

It also uses no directive beyond `SFX`: no `REP`, no `MAP`, nothing for
suggestions. So it is not a model to copy wholesale, but it is proof that
full enumeration is tractable if size is not a constraint, and it would be the
thing to fall back on if two-level chaining ever proved too lossy.

### Hungarian is the one to learn from

Németh's own, and the only other dictionary here that genuinely exploits
two-level suffixation — 26% of its rules carry a continuation flag. It is also
the only one using the full feature set.

| | Hungarian | German | Czech | this |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `COMPOUNDRULE` / `COMPOUNDFLAG` | yes | partial | – | – |
| `KEY` keyboard adjacency | yes | – | yes | – |
| `NOSUGGEST` | yes | yes | yes | – |
| `FORBIDDENWORD` | yes | yes | yes | – |
| `NEEDAFFIX` | yes | yes | – | – |
| `AF` flag aliasing | yes | – | – | – |
| `PHONE` | – | – | – | – |

Of these, `COMPOUND*` is the one whose absence is a real gap in coverage rather
than a missing convenience: Kazakh forms compounds and none are modelled.
`KEY` and `NOSUGGEST` affect suggestion quality only. `AF` is a size
optimisation that would pay off only if entries came to carry many flags, which
at a mean of 1.0 they do not.

Its affix rules are generated by nested m4 macros — Németh says so in
hunspell#90 — which is the same arrangement as here, arrived at twenty years
earlier.

### Nobody measures anything

None of the twelve ship a corpus, a test, or any evaluation. The packages
contain `.aff`, `.dic`, a README, and packaging metadata. There is no standard
benchmark for Hunspell dictionary quality and no published recall figures to
compare against.

## Why recall does not compare across languages

| dictionary | corpus | recall |
|---|---|---|
| `en_US` | `/usr/share/dict/linux.words`, 355,537 words | 21.3% |
| `en_GB` | same | 23.0% |
| this | modern-Kazakh scope, 483,752 forms | 76.7% |

The English dictionary on every Linux desktop scores 21%, because
`linux.words` is a maximalist list heavy with obscure and technical terms and
`en_US` is deliberately scoped to common English in 47,553 entries and 50 affix
rules. The number measures the corpus at least as much as the dictionary.

The same effect is visible inside this project: 76.7% on modern Kazakh, 46.7%
on the historical scope, from one dictionary against one build of one corpus.

## Types are not tokens

Every figure in this repository except the running-text one counts each word
type once, and that overstates how much a dictionary's gaps cost a reader.
Real prose is dominated by a small number of very common words, so missing
`және` is not the same event as missing a word that occurs once.

Both, on the same million words of KazNERD news text:

| | by token | by type |
|---|---|---|
| 2009 release | 82.0% | 44.6% |
| this | 96.6% | 85.6% |

The type figure is the harsher test and the token figure is the honest one for
"what will a user see". They should probably both always be quoted, because
each answers a question the other cannot: type coverage says how complete the
dictionary is, token coverage says how often it will be wrong in front of
someone.

What is left, at 3.4% of tokens: 1.36pp proper names, 1.52pp genuinely unknown
words, 0.54pp one- and two-letter tokens, 0.02pp transcription disfluencies
(`ыы`, `ээ`). Proper names being two fifths of it is worth knowing before
treating the rest as a vocabulary problem — other dictionaries usually handle
names with a separate list, and Hungarian and Czech both use `NOSUGGEST` and
`FORBIDDENWORD` for related purposes.

`көпбалалы` and `көпқабатты` are among the most frequent genuine rejections and
are both compounds in `көп-`, which is the `COMPOUND*` gap showing up in real
text rather than in theory.

## Candidate targets

Numbers that could serve as targets, with what is known about each.

**False accepts, currently 5.9%.** The 2009 release is at 1.8%, and that is the
only external reference point available — it is not a standard, just the thing
being replaced. Reaching 1.8% *and* keeping recall would be the strongest
possible result; nothing establishes it is achievable. This number is also the
one most likely to matter to a user, since a false accept is a silent failure
where a false reject is a visible one.

**Token coverage on running text, currently 96.6%.** Nearest thing to a
user-facing number. The 2009 release is at 82.0%. Two fifths of the remainder
is proper names, so the reachable part is smaller than 3.4% suggests.

**Modern-scope recall, currently 76.7%.** The affix gap is 3,212 forms against
a wordlist gap of 38,766, so this is now almost entirely a vocabulary question.
Unclear what ceiling is meaningful: the residual reduces to 36,918 distinct
stems at 1.22 forms per stem, so there is no small set of additions that moves
it much.

**Suggestion quality, currently unmeasured.** No harness exists, no baseline
exists, and no comparison dictionary publishes one either. Whatever is measured
here would be the project's own definition. The obvious construction is the
existing negatives set — 50,000 forms corrupted from real words in known ways —
scored on whether the original appears in the suggestion list, and at what
rank. That measures exactly what `REP` and `MAP` are for.

**Size, currently 1.6 MB `.dic` and 0.4 MB `.aff`.** Comfortably inside the
range of shipped dictionaries and 22× smaller than Turkish's. Only becomes
interesting if the target is a constrained device.

## Sources not yet drawn on

**apertium-kaz as an analyzer rather than a wordlist.** Currently used as a flat
list of 30,531 entries, discarding its `twol` rules and morphotactics.
Compiling the FST — `hfst` is not installed here but is packaged — would
replace the "shortest headword on the ladder" heuristic in
`tools/mine_residues.py`, which is what admitted `айтды` and `атға` and forced
the minority-vote filter that defends against them. Same licence, already a
dependency.

Its `nla` branch, 175 commits behind master and untouched since 2018-10-07,
holds 4,093 entries master lacks — but they are overwhelmingly surnames
(`абдраимов`, `абишевич`, `абайұлы`).

**KazNLP's analyzer.** Its `sfx` table is already used. The analyzer proper
returns tagged segmentation — `алма_R_ZE сы_S3 н_C4` — which is the layer
information `gen_aff.py` currently infers from bigram adjacency. Its `mdl/`
also holds `stm_lig` and `lkps`, stems with their permitted continuations, i.e.
a real morphotactic model. The repository has no `LICENSE` file; its README
says the tools "will be released under CC-SA-BY or compatible", future tense.

**Kazakh POS models.** 54,416 lexicon entries have no part of speech and
default to the nominal track, and every wrongly-defaulted verb is a
false-accept family of the `адамдың` kind. [aaitim/qaznlp](https://huggingface.co/aaitim/qaznlp)
packages KazBERT + BiLSTM + CRF for Kazakh POS; a
[2025 hybrid analyzer](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/artificial-intelligence/articles/10.3389/frai.2025.1708566/full)
combines FST, CRF and KazRoBERTa against a 150k-sentence annotated corpus.
Neither has been run here.

A language model cannot be part of a shipped dictionary — Hunspell reads static
files — so any model can only act offline, filtering candidate wordlists or
tagging entries.

## The false-accept number needs reading carefully

It is 5.9% here against the 2009 release's 1.8%, and the increase is real: more
affix rules mean more strings accepted, and some of the extra strings are
wrong. But two things distort a naive comparison in opposite directions.

The baseline's figure is partly ignorance rather than precision — it rejects
corruptions of words it does not know, and it does not know 70% of modern
Kazakh. Restricted to pairs where each dictionary knows the *original* word,
the rates are 2.9% and 7.7%: the gap narrows and the increase survives.

The other distortion was in the test. `-сың` and `-сыз` are second person
familiar and second person polite, distinct morphemes, and `negatives.py` had
them as one allomorph family — so `ойнайсың` → `ойнайсыз` counted as a
misspelling when it is a different and perfectly good word. That penalises a
dictionary in proportion to how much of the language it can generate, which is
backwards. It was 18% of the reported rate.

The lesson generalises past this instance: a negative test built by corrupting
real text has to be sure the corruption is not itself a word, and "these two
suffixes rhyme" is not sufficient grounds for believing it.

## Two things that turned out to be traps

**A harness that diagnoses with the component it is testing.** `measure.py`
split rejections into affix and wordlist gaps using the same ladder that could
not strip `-п`, so 6,266 forms with known stems were filed as missing
vocabulary. The conclusion drawn from that — "the affix side is done, 93% is
wordlist" — was wrong, and wrong in the direction that discourages looking.

**A blended corpus.** `Ясауи Хикметтерінің тілі` is 12th-century Chagatai
religious poetry that a Kazakh spellchecker should reject, and the 1966
etymological dictionary explains itself in Russian. Averaging them with the
Academy dictionary produced a single number that concealed a 30-point spread.
